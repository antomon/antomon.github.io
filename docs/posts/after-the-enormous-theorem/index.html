Abstract

On 26 October 2026, Axiom announced a Lean formalization of the classification of finite simple groups, an established theorem whose human proof spans thousands of pages. On the same day, OpenAI announced AI-generated results in harmonic analysis, theoretical computer science, and quantum field theory, together with an experiment in academic stewardship of machine-produced research. On 27 October, Anthropic publicly documented a concurrent, separately developed claim to refute ETH. These events invoke formal verification but expose different problems: reconstructing an existing mathematical edifice and discovering claims that require new mathematical interpretation.

This article analyzes the mathematical substance and the exact documented scope of the results, distinguishes deductive verification from semantic fidelity and physical interpretation, and evaluates the KITP Yang–Mills committee’s staged release against AGMAI’s criticism of privileged access. Its central claim is that generating a checked proof is not equivalent to producing reusable scientific understanding: an adequate research ecosystem must permit independent inspection of formal specifications, repair of inherited arguments, independent verification, explanatory reconstruction, and open scholarly contestation without granting a laboratory an epistemic monopoly.

The article then develops a causal hypothesis for proof abundance. Rather than assuming that discovery is simply proportional to GPU expenditure, it examines learned mathematical competence, agentic search, cheap formal feedback, and reusable proof infrastructure, which together make portions of research effort compute-allocable. This shifts some problem-search capacity from embodied expertise toward institutions that can fund and direct repeated computational attempts. The resulting political economy of research depends not only on who can generate or check theorems, but also on who can allocate mathematical attention and who can independently interpret, preserve, and challenge the results.

Three announcements, two fundamentally different mathematical achievements

On 26 October 2026, Axiom announced what it described as the first complete computer-checked proof of the classification of finite simple groups (CFSG), a theorem established through a vast twentieth-century research programme. The company reported approximately 43.1 million lines of Lean, produced through the coordinated work of AI agents, mathematicians, and engineers over 24 days. The released repository identifies a classification statement, documents its build procedure, and reports a proof without sorry relying on Lean’s standard logical axioms. This is a claim about the reconstruction, checking, and repair of existing mathematics, not primarily about the discovery of the classification itself.12

On that same date, OpenAI announced three ostensibly new results: a proof of Sogge’s local smoothing conjecture for the Euclidean wave equation in all spatial dimensions; a deterministic subexponential-time algorithm that would refute the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH); and existence constructions in four-dimensional quantum field theory, including pure Yang–Mills theory and quantum chromodynamics. It released manuscript and Lean materials for the first two and announced an academic-led process for examining them, while withholding the physics artifacts under a release arrangement delegated to a separate scholarly working group. Those are claims of mathematical discovery, with substantially different degrees of public inspectability and subsequent explanation still in progress.3

The distinction matters more than the apparent coincidence. An agent that translates known mathematics into a mechanically checked library attacks the fragility of a large inherited proof corpus. An agent that proposes a previously unknown theorem attacks the frontier of mathematical search. Neither activity, even when accompanied by a successful Lean check, automatically supplies the third object that makes mathematics cumulative: an intelligible, critically examined, transferable explanation of why the result is true and what it makes possible. The central claim of this article is that verification, discovery, and understanding are distinct outputs, subject to different tests and requiring different infrastructure. It also asks a more causal question: which change in the production of mathematical reasoning is making these outputs appear in such volume, and who can decide where this capacity is deployed?

This argument continues the trajectory of the AI and the transformation of mathematics series. Earlier articles described proof abundance, industrial-scale formalization, repository-scale mathematical production, and the possibility of a mathematical knowledge commons. The near-simultaneous Axiom and OpenAI announcements, followed by Anthropic’s concurrent ETH result, provide an unusually sharp empirical test of that framework: they put both the most impressive kind of archival formalization and some of the most consequential kinds of novel conjecture resolution on the table simultaneously.456

Why the classification of finite simple groups is mathematically enormous

A group is a set with an associative operation, an identity, and inverses. A subgroup N of G is normal when it is invariant under conjugation; such subgroups make quotient groups G/N possible. A nontrivial group is simple if it has no proper nontrivial normal subgroup. For a finite group, the Jordan–Hölder theorem says that the simple factors appearing in a composition series are uniquely determined up to isomorphism and permutation. Classifying finite simple groups therefore gives the atomic types from which every finite group is assembled, though it does not solve the further extension problem of determining all possible groups from their factors.

The classification theorem identifies a finite simple group, up to isomorphism, with a member of a remarkably constrained catalogue: cyclic groups of prime order, alternating groups A_n for n\geq5, the families of simple groups of Lie type over finite fields, or one of 26 sporadic simple groups. The standard presentation counts sixteen Lie-type families, although conventions differ around certain exceptional cases. The crucial assertion is exhaustiveness, not the construction of interesting examples: no other finite simple group can exist. In condensed notation, if \mathcal S is the class of finite simple groups and \mathcal C the explicitly constructed classification list, the characteristic assertion is

\forall G\in\mathcal S,\quad \exists H\in\mathcal C\ \text{such that}\ G\cong H. \tag{1}

The mathematical burden lies in excluding all hypothetical counterexamples, a task that cannot be achieved by enumerating groups up to any fixed order. The Feit–Thompson odd-order theorem, for example, establishes that a finite group of odd order is solvable. Consequently, every nonabelian finite simple group has even order and hence contains an involution, an element of order two. Studying involution centralizers then becomes a general structural instrument for the classification. The resulting theory branches through local subgroup analysis, signalizer functors, recognition theorems, sporadic constructions, and difficult induction arguments. Ronald Solomon’s historical reconstruction explains why the proof emerged as a distributed network of results rather than a single self-contained argument.7

The first-generation proof reached its mature form only after decades of correction. The announcement that the classification was finished in 1983 proved premature; the quasithin case was completed in published form by Michael Aschbacher and Stephen D. Smith in 2004. The subsequent second-generation project reorganized substantial portions of the proof into a more systematic multi-volume account, but was still incomplete at the time of Axiom’s release. The historical difficulty was not merely length. A proof split among hundreds of articles creates a dependency problem: a result may use a lemma in another notation, under a tacit hypothesis, or through a deduction never completely recorded in one place.89

The reach of CFSG follows from the fact that finite groups occur wherever a finite structure admits symmetries. The automorphisms of a finite graph, a combinatorial design, or a code form a finite group, while every finite group can itself be represented as a group of permutations. Questions about such structures can therefore become questions about the subgroups and actions of finite permutation groups. A decisive example is the study of primitive permutation groups, whose actions preserve no nontrivial partition of the underlying set. The O’Nan–Scott analysis reduces their possible structures to a small collection of types involving minimal normal subgroups; for almost-simple types, CFSG identifies the underlying finite simple groups that can occur. This is a route from an exhaustive classification theorem to concrete restrictions on symmetry in algebra and combinatorics, although the classification by itself neither constructs every permitted action nor solves every associated algorithmic problem.10

The classification also resolves universal statements that are not classification statements. The Schreier conjecture, for instance, asserts that for every finite nonabelian simple group S, its outer automorphism group \mathrm{Out}(S)=\mathrm{Aut}(S)/\mathrm{Inn}(S) is solvable. Here \mathrm{Aut}(S) consists of all automorphisms and \mathrm{Inn}(S) of conjugations by elements of S; the quotient describes additional symmetries not arising from conjugation within the group. The claim puts a structural restriction on how a simple group can sit inside a larger group through automorphisms. Its proof is obtained by using the classification and the known automorphism structure of the groups on the list. CFSG thereby becomes an ingredient in deductions about arbitrary finite groups, even when the final statement makes no explicit reference to the classification.11

An even more direct example is the Ore conjecture, proved in 2010 by Liebeck, O’Brien, Shalev, and Tiep: every element s of every finite nonabelian simple group can be expressed as a commutator s=xyx^{-1}y^{-1} for suitable elements x,y of that group. The conclusion is a single, uniform algebraic statement, but its proof requires different techniques across the classified types, including character theory. The classification supplies the exhaustive domain over which the argument must hold; it does not, on its own, provide the commutator representations. This distinction is fundamental to understanding mathematical impact: a long classification can become a prerequisite for short, general theorems whose proofs must nevertheless supply substantial additional reasoning.12

These downstream dependencies, together with the difficulty of maintaining the original proof, determine the significance of Axiom’s effort. Machine-checking CFSG could transform a theorem with unusually expensive human verification into a corpus of precisely stated, linkable, reusable dependencies. Its value would extend beyond the classification itself, because subsequent theorems may invoke CFSG as a deep prerequisite while adding distinct arguments of their own. A reusable formal development would make those prerequisites easier to inspect and potentially reuse in new formal proofs; it would not automatically formalize the conclusions of Schreier, Ore, or every downstream application. It would not replace the original classification as a discovery; it would change the epistemic and technical condition under which the classification can be trusted, investigated, and extended.

What Axiom’s formalization actually contributes

The project’s first contribution is integration. Axiom reports that its agents worked from a dependency blueprint with 108 initial milestones and hundreds of smaller tasks, re-planning the work as unproved prerequisites surfaced. A formalized theorem that proves a desired conclusion only under an added, unproved assumption is a real theorem but not necessarily the theorem needed for CFSG. The distinction is logically elementary and operationally difficult: progress measured by completed files, compiled declarations, or agent activity can increase while the path to the root theorem remains open. Axiom’s report describes explicit integration work to prevent that failure mode.13

The public Lean repository makes the claim more specific. Its stated target is a proposition named ClassificationStatement, asserting that every finite simple group is isomorphic to an explicitly indexed group in the classification list. The project identifies Lean 4.35.0-rc3, Mathlib and TauCeti as foundations, states that the proof is sorry-free, excludes native_decide, and reports only the standard axioms Classical.choice, propext, and Quot.sound. Its documentation provides a lake build procedure; it also acknowledges special build optimizations for the extraordinary scale of the development. These details expose a concrete verification target rather than requiring readers to trust a generic assertion that a model solved the problem.14

The second contribution is mathematical criticism of the existing literature. Axiom reports 34 noteworthy deviations from published arguments, beyond routine typographical or transcription problems. One example involves a quasithin-group lemma for which the agents found a counterexample using \mathrm{SL}_2(5): an equality asserted in the literature failed because the compared quantities took values 1 and 2. Repairing the statement as an inequality was not enough, since later steps had used equality to exclude configurations. Those downstream dependencies also had to be reorganized. This is a concrete instance in which formalization is more than mechanical transcription: it reveals exactly how a seemingly local error propagates through a mathematical argument.15

The most substantial example concerns the Unbalanced Group Theorem (UGT), a structural tool used within the inductive classification. Axiom reports finding a counterexample to a closure step in John Walter’s published route to UGT. The obstruction does not invalidate UGT, which has other proofs, and it does not establish that the classification was previously false. In the example described by Axiom, a field involution acting on \mathrm{PSL}_2(49) has centralizer related to \mathrm{PGL}_2(7); its simple component, \mathrm{PSL}_2(7), falls outside the family whose closure the argument required. The involution is an automorphism of the original group, not an element of that group, a distinction essential to interpreting the counterexample correctly.1617

The repair produced mathematics of its own. Using ideas from Solomon’s theory of maximal 2-components, AxiomProver developed and formalized a preservation argument described as Theorem 2.3, Preservation of simple tops, in the accompanying exposition by Colin Defant and Ken Ono. The paper explains how this result helps route around the obstruction to the UGT argument under the hypotheses required by the classification. Axiom also reports that mathematicians subsequently found a shorter route to the needed conclusion without using that new theorem. This is especially revealing: the formalized argument can be valid and mathematically productive while a later human reconstruction reveals a simpler route. Neither the machine-produced derivation nor the human exposition exhausts the result’s mathematical value.1819

The first available paper is dated 27 October 2026 and is a public preprint, not evidence that the new theorem has already passed independent peer review. Likewise, the existence of an openly accessible Lean repository permits inspection and independent rebuilding but is not, by itself, evidence that an outside team has completed an end-to-end reproduction. This result also extends, rather than duplicates, the phenomenon discussed in this series after Anthropic’s September formalization of Fermat’s Last Theorem: the latter established that a huge existing proof could be industrially translated into Lean, while CFSG adds the challenge of integrating a much more diffuse body of source literature and routing around defects exposed by formalization. The proper reading is therefore strong but bounded: Axiom has published a concrete, inspectable claim to a complete formal CFSG proof, together with specific mathematical repairs; independent reconstruction and long-term scholarly assimilation remain separate tasks.20

OpenAI’s results: new theorems rather than an archival reconstruction

OpenAI’s announcement should not be treated as one undifferentiated breakthrough. It comprises three research programmes, with different mathematical statements, artifact availability, and standards of interpretation. Its distinctive institutional proposal is to regard model-produced manuscripts as raw research data, then ask independent academic groups to turn those materials into explanations that the relevant communities can evaluate.21

Local smoothing, restriction, and the geometry of Kakeya sets

The mathematical result concerns a fundamental question in harmonic analysis and partial differential equations: how strongly can oscillatory waves concentrate in space and time, and what geometric restrictions prevent such concentration from persisting? This question reaches beyond the study of wave propagation because the same mathematical structures govern Fourier restriction, oscillatory integral operators, and the geometry of sets containing line segments in every direction. These connections make the local smoothing conjecture, introduced by Christopher Sogge in 1991, a central organizing problem in modern analysis rather than an isolated estimate.

For suitable Schwartz initial data f on \mathbb R^n, write e^{it|D|}f for the Euclidean half-wave propagator, where |D| is the Fourier multiplier with symbol |\xi|. In the formulation documented for OpenAI’s Lean development, the claimed estimate, for n\geq2, 2\leq p<\infty, and every \varepsilon>0, has the form

\left\|e^{it|D|}f\right\|_{L^p(\mathbb R^n\times[1,2])} \leq C_{n,p,\varepsilon} \left\|f\right\|_{W^{s_p+\varepsilon,p}(\mathbb R^n)}, \qquad s_p=\max\left\{0,\frac{n-1}{2}-\frac np\right\}. \tag{2}

Here W^{s,p} is the L^p-Sobolev space of order s, measuring integrability together with fractional differentiability, and the constant is independent of f; the p=2 case also permits zero Sobolev loss. The estimate controls the spacetime-integrated magnitude of the propagated wave in terms of the regularity of its initial data. It does not by itself assign a universal duration to individual focusing events; the precise gain is an L^p estimate over a fixed time interval. The significance of the estimate is that it controls the spacetime evolution through a comparatively weak regularity requirement on the initial data. In three spatial dimensions, for example, p=3 gives s_p=0, so the asserted estimate requires only an arbitrarily small positive Sobolev loss. The statement concerns the Euclidean half-wave propagator, not arbitrary variable-coefficient geometries or all possible endpoint formulations.22

The connection to Fourier restriction is structural. The Fourier transform decomposes functions into oscillatory components, while restriction theory asks how information concentrated on a curved hypersurface in frequency space controls the resulting function in physical space. For the wave equation, the relevant spacetime frequencies lie on a cone. Localized portions of that cone correspond, through wave-packet decompositions, to packets whose energy propagates along thin tubes. Estimating the combined contribution of many such packets therefore requires understanding how tubes with different directions overlap, concentrate, and interfere. Curvature, transversality, and multiscale geometry become essential, which explains why methods developed for partial differential equations can resolve apparently unrelated questions about geometric sets.

A Kakeya set in \mathbb R^n contains a unit line segment in every direction. Remarkably, such sets may have zero n-dimensional Lebesgue measure, so containing segments in every direction does not force a set to occupy positive volume. The classical Kakeya conjecture instead asserts that every such set must have full Hausdorff dimension n. This is a substantially subtler statement about the behavior of a set at arbitrarily small scales. Its analytic formulation concerns the volume occupied by collections of narrow tubes pointing in many directions: even when those tubes overlap extensively, their directional diversity should prevent their union from becoming too concentrated as their thickness tends to zero. Fourier restriction and local smoothing estimates constrain closely related configurations of wave packets, creating a path from analytic inequalities to geometric dimension bounds.

The consequences documented for OpenAI’s formal development are correspondingly broad. They include the full Hausdorff-dimensional conclusion for Euclidean Kakeya sets in all dimensions n\geq2, maximal Kakeya estimates with arbitrarily small power losses, spherical Fourier-extension estimates, Bochner–Riesz multiplier bounds, and Stein square-function estimates within their specified exponent ranges. The documentation also records quantitative lower bounds on the volume of unions of thin tubes. These are distinct mathematical conclusions, not merely different formulations of the same conjecture. In particular, Fourier restriction estimates control the reconstruction of oscillatory functions from frequency-space data, while Bochner–Riesz estimates address the boundedness of certain truncated Fourier multipliers. Both belong to the mathematical infrastructure used to analyze dispersive evolution, oscillatory phenomena, and related partial differential equations. The documented results do not establish every endpoint estimate or automatically extend to nonlinear or variable-coefficient problems.23

The historical comparison provides an unusually concrete measure of the difficulty involved. In work completed in 2019 and published in the Annals of Mathematics in 2020, Larry Guth, Hong Wang, and Ruixiang Zhang established a sharp square-function estimate for the cone in \mathbb R^3, thereby resolving the local smoothing conjecture for the wave equation in two spatial dimensions. Their argument drew on sophisticated multiscale techniques developed over years of research in harmonic analysis.24

A further breakthrough came in February 2025, when Hong Wang and Joshua Zahl announced a proof of the Kakeya set conjecture in three dimensions, establishing that every Kakeya set in \mathbb R^3 has both Hausdorff and Minkowski dimension three. Their work required new geometric methods for controlling collections of tubes and convex sets across multiple scales. The achievement resolved the three-dimensional instance of a problem that had resisted sustained research for decades, while leaving higher-dimensional Kakeya problems and stronger analytic conjectures unresolved.25

The importance of this achievement received exceptional recognition on 23 July 2026, when Hong Wang was awarded the Fields Medal at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Philadelphia. The International Mathematical Union explicitly recognized her contributions to harmonic analysis and geometric measure theory, including planar local smoothing, Fourier restriction, and the three-dimensional Kakeya problem. The medal recognized this broader body of work, not exclusively the Kakeya theorem, which Wang had proved jointly with Zahl. Nevertheless, the chronology is striking: just three months before OpenAI’s announcement, one of mathematics’ highest distinctions had recognized a mathematician whose achievements included resolving a particular dimensional instance of a problem that OpenAI now claims to settle in every Euclidean dimension.26

The comparison should not be interpreted as a simple ranking of mathematical achievements. Wang and Zahl’s proof provided a human-developed geometric argument whose techniques could be examined, reformulated, and extended through established mathematical practice. OpenAI’s claimed result addresses a broader analytic statement from which several geometric and Fourier-analytic conclusions follow, but the significance of the proposed auxiliary partial differential equation, the generality of its underlying mechanism, and the correspondence between the formalized statements and their intended mathematical meanings still require independent mathematical examination. Nor does a stronger theorem automatically supply a more illuminating explanation of the phenomena it governs.

Nevertheless, the difference in scope is mathematically substantial. Whereas the Guth–Wang–Zhang and Wang–Zahl breakthroughs resolved particular dimensional cases or consequences through specialized techniques, OpenAI reports a single approach covering Euclidean local smoothing across all spatial dimensions n\geq2 and yielding several families of consequences. If the proof and formal specifications withstand independent scrutiny, the result would change the structure of harmonic analysis by replacing multiple outstanding dimensional problems with consequences of a more general principle. OpenAI reports that its model generated the natural-language argument on 18 October 2026 and completed the associated Lean formalization the following day, using a novel auxiliary partial differential equation whose conceptual significance remains to be reconstructed by the mathematical community.27

This distinction is central to the broader transformation examined in this article. The historical results emerged through prolonged specialization, the accumulation of technical methods, and the development of mathematical intuition across a research community. The AI-generated result, if validated, would illustrate a different mode of mathematical production: a general theorem with several major downstream consequences produced rapidly as a computational artifact. Its eventual scientific value, however, depends not only on the correctness of the formal derivation but also on whether mathematicians can recover the organizing principles, identify which assumptions are essential, and transform the proof into methods that support further research. The production of a theorem and its incorporation into mathematical understanding are different achievements.

The Exponential Time Hypothesis and the meaning of a subexponential algorithm

The Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) concerns one of the most fundamental questions in theoretical computer science: whether certain computational problems intrinsically require resources that grow exponentially with the size of their inputs. Its importance derives not only from the difficulty of Boolean satisfiability but also from its role as a foundational assumption used to establish conditional lower bounds throughout algorithm design. A refutation would therefore affect an extensive network of theoretical results, even if the algorithm responsible for it proved unusable on realistic instances.

Boolean satisfiability (SAT) asks whether there exists an assignment of truth values to variables that makes a propositional formula true. In conjunctive normal form (CNF), the formula is a conjunction of clauses, each consisting of a disjunction of literals. The special case 3-SAT restricts each clause to at most three literals. SAT is the canonical NP-complete decision problem: a proposed satisfying assignment can be checked in polynomial time, while every problem in NP can be transformed into SAT through a polynomial-time reduction. This universality explains why SAT is central both to complexity theory and to practical applications such as hardware verification, model checking, constraint solving, software analysis, and automated planning.2829

A closely related but more demanding problem is model counting, denoted \#\mathrm{SAT}, which asks for the exact number of satisfying assignments rather than merely whether one exists. For a Boolean formula F over n variables, define

\#F = \left|\left\{ x\in\{0,1\}^n : F(x)=1 \right\}\right|.

Ordinary satisfiability is recovered by testing whether \#F>0, but determining the exact count contains considerably more information. Model counting is relevant to probabilistic inference, reliability analysis, quantitative verification, and design-space exploration, where knowing that a valid configuration exists may be insufficient: one may also need to determine how many configurations are valid, what fraction of possible configurations satisfy a property, or how the solution space changes under additional constraints. General exact propositional model counting is \#P-complete. OpenAI’s reported result is correspondingly stronger in its output requirement than deciding satisfiability on the same fixed-width formulas, since the number of solutions determines whether any solution exists.30

The ETH, introduced by Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi and developed further with Francis Zane, asserts in one standard formulation that 3-SAT cannot be solved in time 2^{o(n)}, where n is the number of variables. Here o(n) denotes any function whose ratio to n tends to zero as n tends to infinity. The conjecture therefore excludes every subexponential worst-case algorithm for 3-SAT, not just polynomial-time algorithms. It does not assert that all difficult instances require exhaustive enumeration, that every formula is hard, or that successful SAT solving is computationally impossible in practice. Rather, it places a proposed asymptotic lower bound on the resources required by algorithms that must handle every instance.31

OpenAI claims a deterministic algorithm that exactly counts the satisfying assignments of fixed-width k-CNF formulas. The scope note accompanying its Lean formalization certifies a bound of the form

T_k(n,L) \leq (L+1)^{O_k(1)} 2^{\,O_k\!\left(n(\log\log n)^2/\sqrt{\log n}\right)}, \tag{3}

for fixed k\geq2, where L is the bit length of the specified canonical encoding. The documented machine uses polynomially bounded tape space. Since (\log\log n)^2/\sqrt{\log n}\to0, the exponential factor is 2^{o(n)}. For conventional fixed-width instances whose encoding length is polynomially bounded in n, the polynomial prefactor does not change this asymptotic conclusion. Thus, if the machine, encoding, and formal statements correspond to their intended conventional meanings, the result contradicts ETH.32

An important distinction is easily lost in headlines: the natural-language manuscript reports a sharper exponent containing one power of \log\log n, while the public scope note specifies the weaker squared-log-log exponent for the Lean development. The manuscript also reports a stronger estimate for bounded-density instances. The formally documented weaker bound remains sufficient for the qualitative conclusion, but not every quantitative refinement in the manuscript should be attributed to the machine-checked theorem.3334

Why this does not resolve P versus NP

The relationship with P versus NP requires particular care because the two questions concern different levels of algorithmic efficiency. The class \mathrm P consists of decision problems solvable in polynomial time in the input length, whereas \mathrm{NP} consists of decision problems for which a positive answer has a certificate verifiable in polynomial time. The P versus NP problem asks whether these classes are equal. Because 3-SAT is NP-complete, a polynomial-time algorithm for 3-SAT would establish \mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}, while proving that no polynomial-time algorithm exists for 3-SAT would establish \mathrm P\ne\mathrm{NP}.35

Subexponential complexity is a different property. A running time such as 2^{n/\sqrt{\log n}} is subexponential in n, because the exponent grows more slowly than linearly, but remains superpolynomial: for every fixed c>0, it eventually exceeds n^c. Consequently, even a fully verified 2^{o(n)} algorithm for 3-SAT would leave open the possibility that solving the problem requires superpolynomial time. The logical relationship is asymmetric:

\mathrm P=\mathrm{NP} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \neg\mathrm{ETH},

but the converse implication is not established. Refuting ETH does not prove either \mathrm P=\mathrm{NP} or \mathrm P\ne\mathrm{NP}. Likewise, the existence of a subexponential algorithm for an NP-complete problem does not automatically provide subexponential algorithms, measured in their original input sizes, for all problems in NP. A general polynomial-time reduction may increase the number of variables polynomially rather than linearly, potentially destroying the subexponential bound when expressed in the original problem’s parameter.36

This is why P versus NP remains a deeper unresolved classification problem. An affirmative answer would imply polynomial-time algorithms for every NP decision problem, with extensive consequences for combinatorial optimization, verification, and computational search, although polynomial-time worst-case complexity would not guarantee practical efficiency. A negative answer would establish an unconditional boundary between efficient verification and efficient solution. ETH proposes a considerably stronger kind of hardness for particular problems, namely exponential rather than merely superpolynomial worst-case complexity. Its refutation would remove that stronger boundary without resolving the more general separation.37

Why the consequences extend beyond satisfiability

The immediate theoretical impact concerns fine-grained complexity, which studies the precise resource requirements of computational problems rather than classifying them only as polynomial or NP-hard. ETH and its stronger relative, the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH), have been used to derive conditional lower bounds for graph problems, combinatorial optimization, dynamic algorithms, computational geometry, string processing, and parameterized algorithms. A typical result establishes that a faster algorithm for one problem would imply an algorithm contradicting ETH or SETH. Such results have allowed researchers to explain why existing algorithms might be close to optimal even when unconditional lower bounds remain beyond current proof techniques.3839

SETH asserts, roughly, that as clause width increases, the exponent of the best general k-SAT algorithm cannot remain uniformly bounded away from that of exhaustive search. The fixed-width subexponential algorithm reported by OpenAI would refute both ETH and SETH, as well as the corresponding exact-counting exponential-time hypothesis, \#\mathrm{ETH}. These consequences follow because exact counting decides satisfiability and because the fixed-width algorithms apply across every fixed k. They do not imply that every conjecture historically motivated by SETH is false. A statement proved in the form “if SETH holds, then no algorithm of type A exists” remains a valid conditional theorem; what disappears is its ability to establish hardness if SETH itself is false.40

The implications are not confined to conditional lower bounds. OpenAI’s manuscript derives concrete algorithmic corollaries, including deterministic subexponential-time procedures for counting independent sets in graphs, counting proper graph colorings for a fixed number of colors, and counting perfect matchings. If a graph has v vertices and e edges, the reported consequences include 2^{o(v)} bounds for independent-set counting and fixed-color graph coloring, and a 2^{o(v+e)} bound for counting perfect matchings. These conclusions arise through reductions that preserve sufficiently tight relationships between the original combinatorial parameters and the number of Boolean variables.41

The reductions illuminate the mechanism. An independent set is a collection of vertices with no adjacent pair. Assign one Boolean variable x_u to every vertex u; for each edge \{u,w\}, the clause (\neg x_u\vee\neg x_w) forbids selecting both endpoints. Satisfying assignments then correspond bijectively to independent sets. Exact counting of the resulting CNF therefore gives exact counting of independent sets. This is not merely the loss of a conjectural lower bound: it is a constructive transfer of the reported SAT algorithm to another well-defined computational problem. Suitable count-preserving reductions also yield the graph-coloring and matching corollaries reported in the manuscript; for each reduction, the number of Boolean variables and the encoding cost must be controlled to retain a subexponential running time in the original graph parameter.42

The distinction between reductions also identifies where immediate consequences end. Many hardness arguments reduce SAT to a target problem: a hypothetical fast solution to that target would yield a fast SAT algorithm. A faster SAT algorithm cannot simply reverse this implication. To accelerate a particular target problem using the new result, one instead needs an efficient reduction from the target problem to fixed-width SAT, controlling the number of Boolean variables, the input length, and, for counting applications, preservation of the number of solutions. OpenAI’s manuscript gives bounded-set-size Set Splitting as an example where such a direct reduction is available. This directionality of reductions is one reason why a general complexity-theoretic breakthrough need not automatically yield faster algorithms for every problem whose hardness had previously been argued from ETH.43

A second limitation concerns the remaining assumptions of fine-grained complexity. Several conditional lower bounds for edit distance, longest common subsequence, dynamic time warping, regular-expression matching, and geometric problems were developed from SETH. Some of these results, however, also follow from alternative hypotheses, such as the Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture, which excludes strongly subquadratic algorithms for detecting orthogonal pairs of Boolean vectors. Although SETH implies that conjecture, falsifying SETH does not imply its negation. Consequently, a claimed ETH refutation does not automatically yield subquadratic string-matching algorithms, faster geometric procedures, or the collapse of every existing fine-grained lower-bound argument.44

The relationships among these results are best understood as a network of logical implications rather than a single progression from mathematical discovery to faster software. As illustrated in Figure 1, a valid subexponential exact-counting algorithm would immediately yield a subexponential decision procedure for 3-SAT, contradicting ETH and SETH. Appropriate reductions would also transfer the improvement to certain combinatorial counting problems, while lower bounds whose significance depends on ETH or SETH would require reassessment. Yet neither the P versus NP problem nor the performance of practical SAT solvers is resolved by these deductions. The diagram therefore distinguishes mathematical consequences that follow from the claimed theorem from additional advances requiring independent theoretical or engineering work.

The distance between theoretical and practical impact

SAT technology already plays an important role in computer science and engineering. Industrial SAT solvers support verification of hardware designs, analysis of software properties, planning, and numerous constraint-solving tasks. Exact model counting, although computationally more demanding, is used in probabilistic reasoning, reliability estimation, and quantitative verification. These applications establish why improved algorithms for satisfiability and counting could eventually have practical value, but their existence does not demonstrate that either newly proposed algorithm will improve current implementations.4546

The distinction is between asymptotic possibility and effective computational performance. Contemporary SAT solvers exploit clause learning, propagation, preprocessing, instance structure, and carefully engineered heuristics. A new algorithm with a superior asymptotic bound may still have enormous constants, expensive intermediate representations, or a crossover point far beyond any physically realizable computation. It may also require restructuring that destroys the favorable properties of instances generated by existing applications. In particular, a faster exact-counting algorithm in the asymptotic sense need not be a competitive replacement for a specialized decision SAT solver.

OpenAI explicitly acknowledges this gap in its announcement:

“Finding such algorithms, and turning theoretical advances into practical improvements, will require significant further research.”47

The statement is significant because it separates three developments that should not be conflated: disproving a proposed complexity lower bound, discovering faster algorithms for other problems through mathematically valid reductions, and engineering algorithms that outperform established software on useful workloads. The first concerns the logical structure of complexity theory; the second concerns the transfer of mathematical methods; the third requires algorithm design, implementation, benchmarking, and empirical evaluation. None follows automatically from the preceding stage.

OpenAI published both a natural-language argument and a Lean development, while a group led by Russell Impagliazzo, Ramamohan Paturi, and Ryan Williams is organizing critical analysis of the result. That group’s announced role is to examine and explain the claim, not to provide an already completed certification. If the result survives such examination, its immediate importance would be a revision of a central assumption about computational hardness and the discovery of new possibilities for algorithm design. Determining which of those possibilities ultimately become useful software would remain a separate research programme.48

Independent ETH discoveries and the emerging competition between AI laboratories

Anthropic’s release makes the complexity-theoretic result more consequential for this article than a single-laboratory case study. On 27 October 2026, Anthropic deposited A 2^{o(n)} Time Algorithm for 3-SAT on Hexagon, a preprint repository rather than a peer-review venue. Its provenance statement says that an internal Claude research model found the core algorithm on 16 October, generating roughly 14 million output tokens without human input, before researchers worked with Claude to improve the manuscript. OpenAI independently dates its own natural-language ETH argument to 16 October, reports a Lean formalization on 17 October, and published the results on 26 October, one day before Anthropic’s Hexagon deposit. Anthropic explicitly describes the discoveries as independent and concurrent. This is unusually specific provenance for two exceptionally similar claims, although provenance supplied by competing organizations is not equivalent to external reconstruction of their respective discovery histories.4950

The mathematical comparison is substantive. Anthropic’s main theorem claims that, for a k-CNF formula with n occurring variables and m clauses, a single deterministic algorithm can compute the exact number of satisfying assignments within

T(k,n,m)\leq 2^{\,O\!\left(\min\left\{\frac{k^2n\log\log n}{\sqrt{\log n}},\,\frac{km}{\sqrt{\log m}}\right\}\right)}. \tag{4}

The constants in the exponent are asserted to be absolute, with the precise finite-input restrictions specified in the Lean statement. For each fixed k, this is subexponential in n; the second branch is especially informative for formulas with relatively few clauses. The algorithm reexpresses exact satisfiability counting as sums of products or tensor-network contractions, then uses a signed expansion of crossing constraints to permit advantageous contraction orders. An algebraic decomposition related to the classical eight-square identity, followed by a recursive procedure and count-preserving sparsification, supplies the nontrivial saving. This is not merely an alternative presentation of brute force, nor does the paper rely on a heuristic SAT solver. It is a proposed exact algorithm with a structurally explicit complexity analysis.51

The two approaches also differ in their mathematical organization. OpenAI’s manuscript relies on algebraic transformations, interpolation, and methods for evaluating structured counting expressions, whereas Anthropic’s construction emphasizes tensor-network representations, algebraic identities, and contraction strategies. The existence of distinct candidate mechanisms for overcoming the same asymptotic barrier is important independently of the commercial competition: it raises the possibility that the barrier was associated with limitations of known algorithmic representations rather than a fundamental exponential lower bound. Whether the two constructions can be reduced to a common mathematical principle, or whether they exploit genuinely different sources of computational savings, remains a substantive question for subsequent research.5253

The results should nevertheless not be ranked by reading their exponents as though they were benchmark scores. Anthropic’s reported Lean-checked n-dependent exponent contains one factor of \log\log n, while OpenAI’s public scope document establishes an exponent containing (\log\log n)^2; OpenAI’s natural-language manuscript itself gives the sharper single-log-log expression, and for bounded-density inputs it describes a bound without that factor. Thus the publicly documented formal artifacts have different quantitative strengths, and the hypotheses, encodings, machine models, and constants also differ. Neither comparison establishes that one construction is faster on realistic instances. Both purport to cross the same asymptotic dividing line: exact counting for fixed-width CNF, and consequently deciding 3-SAT, in subexponential time.5455

Anthropic provides unusually useful evidence about the formal boundary. Appendix E gives readable Lean declarations for ETHIsFalse and MainTheorem and reports that proofs were accepted by the Lean 4 kernel, replayed by leanchecker, and checked through Comparator using the separately implemented nanoda kernel. The development is reported to comprise 1,050 Lean modules and to use no axioms beyond propext, Classical.choice, and Quot.sound. Crucially, the appendix itself says that no person was then known to have checked the proof’s mathematical meaning, that AI models performed the comparison between formal and printed arguments, and that two translation steps—from conventional formula representations to the specified machine inputs, and from the formal machine to a word-RAM interpretation—are not themselves included in the Lean proof. The standalone MainResults.lean listing defines the target propositions rather than containing the proofs; their derivations are in the separate development. These qualifications make the claim inspectable without turning kernel acceptance into human certification.56

The claimed consequences are potentially major but sharply delimited. If confirmed, Anthropic’s algorithm also falsifies ETH and SETH and requires the conditional hardness literature to revisit assumptions that have supported lower bounds for decades. The result is relevant to the same family of computational tasks discussed in the preceding section: satisfiability, exact counting, graph algorithms, constraint systems, and problems connected through fine-grained reductions. Its mathematical contribution is nevertheless an existence theorem for a particular asymptotic algorithm, not a demonstration that practical implementations can solve previously intractable instances. It does not establish \mathrm P=\mathrm{NP}, refute the Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture, or make every theorem previously conditional on ETH logically false.57

The scale of the practical limitation is particularly revealing. Anthropic estimates that for sparse formulas with approximately five clauses per variable, its proven upper bound would not beat exhaustive enumeration until the number of variables exceeded roughly 10^{10000}; for more general instances the quoted crossover is even more remote. This is an asymptotic estimate, not an empirical benchmark, and its numerical value depends on the constants and implementation model used in the analysis. Nevertheless, it demonstrates why an asymptotically subexponential algorithm can possess essentially no direct computational utility at attainable scales. The relevant mathematical achievement is the violation of a proposed lower bound, not the immediate feasibility of executing the algorithm.58

This gap also helps explain why the two independent discoveries should be evaluated on different timescales. In the short term, their significance lies in the possible refutation of ETH, the resulting changes to fine-grained complexity, and the opportunity to compare different algebraic approaches to exact counting. In the longer term, researchers may discover simplified constructions, stronger quantitative bounds, new reductions, or practical algorithmic components derived from the original techniques. Such developments are possible consequences, not results already established. Historically, important theoretical algorithms have sometimes influenced practical computing through concepts that survive after their original implementations have been substantially transformed; whether this occurs here is an empirical and mathematical question that cannot be answered from the present preprints alone.

Does this establish a race between OpenAI and Anthropic? There are two evidentially different senses of that word. The near-simultaneous, internally attributed discoveries and successive public releases are direct evidence of parallel frontier activity; because both laboratories are developing proprietary research models, competitive incentives are a plausible part of the institutional setting. They do not establish that one model copied the other, that either company knew the other’s result on 16 October, or that Anthropic published on 27 October because OpenAI published on 26 October. Inferring any of those causal claims requires information not contained in the public records.5960

The broader significance concerns the organization of scientific discovery. Two laboratories report finding independent solutions to a major complexity-theoretic problem on the same day, using substantial computational resources directed toward mathematical research. If their claims withstand verification, the episode would illustrate how computationally organized theorem search can produce concurrent results that would ordinarily require prolonged human investigation. Yet the production of independent proofs does not automatically produce independent understanding: the mathematical community must still reconstruct their mechanisms, assess the formal encodings, establish the precise consequences, and determine whether either technique can be generalized.

The resulting tension is central to the transformation examined in this article. Research generation may increasingly be parallelized and competitively funded, while validation, explanation, and community assimilation remain slower and less directly scalable activities. The scientific challenge is not merely to increase the number of correct mathematical artifacts, but to ensure that a growing supply of such artifacts becomes a reproducible, intelligible, and cumulatively useful body of knowledge.

Four-dimensional quantum field theory: from mathematical existence to physical understanding

The third announcement extends the significance of AI-generated mathematical discovery into theoretical physics, where the relationship between mathematical rigor and empirical success has historically been more complicated than in pure mathematics or theoretical computer science. Quantum field theory (QFT) provides the framework underlying the Standard Model of particle physics and many other physical phenomena, yet the empirical success of its predictions does not mean that every theory employed by physicists has been constructed as a mathematically well-defined object. In particular, the rigorous construction of interacting quantum field theories in four spacetime dimensions has remained one of the central challenges of mathematical physics. The distinction is between a theory that yields extraordinarily accurate physical predictions through established calculational methods and a theory whose existence and fundamental mathematical properties can be derived without relying on uncontrolled formal manipulations.61

Yang–Mills theory, introduced by Chen Ning Yang and Robert Mills in 1954, describes gauge fields whose interactions are governed by non-Abelian symmetry groups. Unlike electromagnetism’s Abelian gauge structure, non-Abelian gauge fields can interact directly with one another. This feature is essential to quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory of the strong interaction, whose gauge group is SU(3) and whose fundamental degrees of freedom include gluons and quarks. Pure Yang–Mills theory contains the self-interacting gauge fields without dynamical matter fermions, whereas QCD couples those fields to quarks. The distinction matters because mathematical results established for pure Yang–Mills do not automatically extend to QCD, even though the two theories share important structures.62

The mathematical difficulty arises from the coexistence of two scales with very different behavior. At high energies, asymptotic freedom makes the strong interaction weaker, allowing perturbative calculations to describe many experimentally accessible processes with remarkable accuracy. At low energies, however, the interaction becomes strong and perturbative expansions cease to provide a generally adequate description. This is precisely the regime in which quarks and gluons form hadrons, the composite particles that include protons and neutrons. Understanding how a theory formulated in terms of fundamental gauge fields produces the observed spectrum of strongly interacting particles requires non-perturbative methods. The difficulty is therefore not merely to calculate additional terms in an expansion but to control the theory in a regime where the expansion itself is insufficient.6364

Why proving existence would matter for physics

Kenneth Wilson’s lattice formulation offers a non-perturbative starting point. Rather than defining fields immediately at every point of continuous spacetime, one introduces a discrete lattice with spacing a, allowing the relevant integrals and correlation functions to be defined with an ultraviolet cutoff. Numerical lattice QCD has developed into an extensive research programme, producing quantitative results for hadron masses, decay constants, quark masses, strong-interaction parameters, and other physically observable quantities. These calculations are not hypothetical consequences of the AI announcement: they are established accomplishments of computational particle physics, obtained using Monte Carlo simulation, numerical analysis, and increasingly powerful computing infrastructure.65

A finite lattice, however, is not the desired continuum quantum field theory. Its spacing introduces an artificial short-distance scale, while its finite volume restricts long-distance phenomena. Recovering the intended physical theory requires controlling the continuum limit a\to0 and the infinite-volume limit L\to\infty, where L denotes a characteristic spatial extent. The underlying parameters must be renormalized appropriately, and the limiting construction must satisfy the properties required of a meaningful quantum field theory. These include the appropriate spacetime symmetries and, in a Euclidean construction intended to describe relativistic quantum physics, conditions that permit reconstruction of a physically admissible Hilbert-space theory. A rigorous construction must therefore justify the existence and properties of the limits, not merely demonstrate that simulations at successively finer lattice spacings appear to converge.6667

The renormalization group supplies a possible route through this difficulty. By integrating out degrees of freedom at successive scales, it describes how effective interactions change as the resolution of a physical system changes. Wilson’s approach made this principle central to modern theoretical physics, while Tadeusz Balaban developed rigorous renormalization-group methods for lattice gauge theories during the 1980s. In the October 2026 statement published by the Kavli Institute for Theoretical Physics (KITP), the Yang–Mills shepherding committee explicitly identifies Balaban’s work as a foundational ingredient in OpenAI’s claimed construction. The committee attributes the claimed continuum construction to a line of argument drawing on Balaban’s methods; it does not report that the independent review of the construction is complete.68

If established under the appropriate mathematical and physical assumptions, such a result would be significant for reasons extending beyond the existence theorem itself. It would provide a rigorous connection between a non-perturbative lattice construction and a continuum quantum field theory, thereby strengthening the foundation on which physical calculations are interpreted. The construction could also supply reusable techniques for investigating correlation functions, spectra, and other properties that remain difficult to control rigorously. The applicability of those techniques to further problems would, however, require additional theorems: the existence of a quantum field theory does not by itself establish effective numerical algorithms, quantitative error bounds for simulations, or formulas for experimentally measured quantities.6970

This is the essential difference between the impact of the ETH result and the potential impact of Yang–Mills existence. In complexity theory, a subexponential algorithm directly changes an asserted asymptotic computational bound, even if no practical implementation becomes competitive. In quantum field theory, a rigorous existence proof would instead establish that a physically motivated mathematical construction survives the removal of its regulators and satisfies the required axioms. Its immediate contribution would be to the logical foundations of an existing physical framework; subsequent improvements in predictive calculation would depend on additional mathematical and computational developments.

What the existence claim does not establish

OpenAI reports existence proofs for pure Yang–Mills theory, quantum chromodynamics, and certain interacting gauge–fermion theories in four dimensions. It additionally claims a construction for a unified completion incorporating the gauge fields and fermions of the Standard Model. The announcement identifies this as a particular grand-unified theory, not as a demonstration that the experimentally established Standard Model is mathematically complete at arbitrarily short distances. Its reported Lean formalization specifically concerns pure Yang–Mills existence, rather than every claimed QCD and unified-theory result. The underlying physics manuscripts and Lean development were not made publicly available with the announcement, so their precise definitions, assumptions, and proofs cannot presently be independently reproduced from the released materials.71

The distinction between existence and the mass gap is especially important. The Clay Mathematics Institute’s Yang–Mills Millennium Prize problem asks for the construction of a nontrivial four-dimensional quantum Yang–Mills theory for any compact simple gauge group, together with a proof that the theory possesses a positive mass gap. In a Hamiltonian formulation with vacuum energy normalized to zero, assuming the existence of a vacuum vector with H\Omega=0, the mass-gap property requires a number \Delta>0 such that

\operatorname{spec}(H)\cap(0,\Delta)=\varnothing, \tag{5}

where H is the physical Hamiltonian and \operatorname{spec}(H) denotes its spectrum. In other words, the vacuum is separated from positive-energy excitations by a strictly positive energy threshold. This is a spectral statement about the quantum theory, not merely an assertion that the classical fields or the particles appearing in a Lagrangian possess explicit mass terms. Proving it requires substantially more information about the theory than establishing its mathematical existence.72

The mass gap is physically significant because the strongly interacting theory is expected to exhibit characteristic positive mass scales even though its classical gauge fields are massless. Closely related, but mathematically distinct, is confinement, the phenomenon whereby colored quarks and gluons do not appear as isolated observable particles. The existence of a mass gap and the confinement of color are not interchangeable statements, nor does a proof of either automatically establish the other without additional assumptions and arguments. Both concern the non-perturbative regime, where direct perturbative reasoning is insufficient and where decades of lattice computations have provided evidence that is much stronger than the available general existence proofs.7374

The KITP committee explicitly states that confinement and the mass gap remain open challenges. Accordingly, the announced existence construction does not resolve the complete Clay Millennium Prize problem. It would also not establish that every property of physical QCD follows from the construction, that the Standard Model is ultraviolet complete, or that the proposed grand-unified theory describes nature. These are separate mathematical and empirical obligations. A mathematically consistent theory can fail to reproduce experimental observations; conversely, the experimental accuracy of a physical model does not automatically provide a rigorous proof that its continuum construction exists.7576

Unlike the ETH case, where the claimed algorithm entails a contradiction of a precisely formulated complexity hypothesis, the physics claims concern several logically distinct objectives. The proposed existence proof would establish a foundation on which further mathematical investigations might proceed, but it would not itself imply the unresolved spectral, confinement, or phenomenological conclusions.

What the Yang–Mills committee actually says

An important primary source changes how OpenAI’s release decision should be interpreted. In a two-page statement dated October 2026 and hosted by KITP, a committee comprising Michael R. Douglas, David J. Gross (chair), Arthur Jaffe, Igor R. Klebanov, and Nikita Nekrasov explains that OpenAI asked Gross on 16 October 2026 to take custody of the proof and decide how to shepherd its release. Gross subsequently selected the other members. The committee states that it has no responsibility to OpenAI and that its role is to serve the academic community, while seeking to avoid the dissemination of incorrect results and failures of scholarly attribution. These statements establish the committee’s declared remit and intended independence; they do not establish that its members have completed a mathematical verification of the claim.77

The statement places the announcement in the context of decades of theoretical and computational physics. It describes Wilson’s lattice regularization, the development of lattice gauge theory as a major computational research programme, and Balaban’s rigorous renormalization-group work. The committee considers the reported construction a potentially important step toward placing quantum field theory on a rigorous mathematical footing. However, its language must be read together with its explicit acknowledgment of the unresolved mass gap and confinement problems. The claimed advance concerns the existence and continuum construction of the theory, not a completed explanation of the entire non-perturbative phenomenology of the strong interaction.78

Most importantly, the committee sets out a staged validation and release protocol. Its first objective is to check that the definitions and theorem statement correspond to the intended objects of physics and mathematics and that OpenAI’s Lean formalization agrees with the informal argument. Once that validation is completed, the committee says it will release the Lean proof. It then intends to develop a deeper mathematical and physical interpretation, eventually publishing an assessment together with the 1,147-page LLM-generated proofs. This is a more precise description of the delay than a general appeal to the difficulty of reviewing a lengthy manuscript: it identifies verification of the semantic correspondence between formal mathematical objects and their intended physical interpretation as an explicit prerequisite to disclosure.79

The distinction is substantively important. A proof assistant checks that a derivation is valid relative to its formal definitions, axioms, and inference rules. It does not independently determine whether those definitions encode the intended gauge theory, whether the assumptions conceal an essential physical limitation, or whether the resulting construction satisfies the conditions demanded by a particular problem in mathematical physics. For example, an existence proof for a regulated or restricted theory is not automatically an existence proof for the desired continuum theory in infinite volume. The committee’s stated priorities therefore address a genuine epistemic risk rather than a merely editorial concern.

Nevertheless, the procedure is institutionally contestable. If the formal definitions fail to capture the intended physical theory, premature claims of success could mislead the mathematical and physics communities; the committee’s caution responds to that possibility. On the other hand, withholding the proof prevents other researchers from inspecting precisely those definitions, assumptions, and dependencies needed to identify such a mismatch. The absence of publicly available artifacts is therefore an evidentiary limitation: a review plan, however carefully conceived, cannot substitute for a proof that others can examine and reproduce.

This is the substance of the disagreement with the Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence (AGMAI), which called on OpenAI on 26 October 2026 to release the natural-language and Lean outputs immediately. AGMAI argues that independent understanding should emerge from open and equitable access rather than through the privileged designation of selected researchers. Its position does not deny the importance of expert examination; it challenges whether exclusive control of the underlying artifacts is necessary to perform that examination. The committee’s procedure and AGMAI’s demand for openness respond to different risks, and neither position eliminates the need for semantic validation and scientific interpretation.8081

Implications for the transformation of science

The potential scientific consequences are therefore wider than the formal existence claim, but also more conditional. In mathematical physics, a validated construction could transform a body of physically successful but partly non-rigorous reasoning into a collection of theorems with explicit hypotheses and reusable mathematical dependencies. That transformation could support further investigation of confinement, correlation functions, spectral properties, and the relationship between lattice and continuum formulations. Whether it would lead to new quantitative predictions, more efficient simulation algorithms, or experimentally distinguishable physical conclusions would depend on subsequent advances. The existing success of lattice QCD demonstrates that useful physical predictions can precede complete axiomatic construction; the converse is equally important, because axiomatic construction does not automatically produce useful predictions.8283

The case also exposes two different meanings of compute-constrained science. Lattice QCD has long been computationally constrained because numerical estimates of physical observables require costly simulations, increasingly large lattices, and careful control of statistical and systematic errors. AI-assisted mathematical research introduces a different computational activity: searching for proofs that establish the existence and properties of the objects being simulated. These computational regimes are complementary but not interchangeable. More resources devoted to theorem search may yield a rigorous foundation without reducing the cost of numerical simulation; more resources devoted to numerical simulation may improve empirical precision without proving the existence theorem. The transformation becomes scientifically consequential when the two processes can inform one another through reusable methods and validated mathematical results.

This difference also helps explain why the consequences of AI-generated mathematics may extend beyond mathematics itself. A general theorem in harmonic analysis changes what can be established about waves and geometric concentration; a subexponential algorithm changes a proposed boundary on computation; A validated rigorous Yang–Mills construction would strengthen the mathematical foundations of a central physical framework without automatically establishing the corresponding results for QCD or the Standard Model. The common development is not simply that AI systems produce longer or more difficult proofs. It is that computationally generated mathematical results may begin to alter the conceptual foundations on which other sciences build their explanations and predictions.

Yet the Yang–Mills episode demonstrates why proof generation, formal verification, physical interpretation, and empirical validation cannot be collapsed into a single notion of scientific discovery. A mathematical construction may be correct within its formal system without representing the intended physical object. It may represent that object faithfully without proving all the physical properties that motivated its study. And it may establish important physical properties without yielding immediately more accurate or less expensive predictions. The transformation of science will depend not only on producing proofs at greater scale, but on developing institutions and methods capable of connecting those proofs to the explanatory and experimental obligations of the disciplines in which they are intended to matter.

What formal verification does—and does not—certify

The shared term Lean-verified conceals several logically distinct questions. Given a formal environment \Gamma, a proof term p, and a precisely encoded proposition P, a sound type-checking kernel can establish the judgment \Gamma\vdash p:P. This is a statement about deduction from the definitions, axioms, and previously admitted constructions in \Gamma. The kernel does not independently know whether P is the mathematician’s intended theorem, whether an encoding corresponds to a physical observable, or whether an auxiliary assumption was introduced merely to make the target provable.

A rigorous assurance procedure therefore has to inspect at least the declared target and its mathematical interpretation; the transitive axiom and dependency closure; the completeness and origin of computational certificates; reproducible build instructions and toolchain versions; and the provenance of the objects being compared with the published claim. The logical check is indispensable, but its success and the success of those surrounding obligations are not synonymous. Axiom’s emphasis on the actual ClassificationStatement, and OpenAI’s publication of Lean scope documents and comparator targets, are responses to this problem—not automatic proof that every semantic mapping has already been audited by independent specialists.848586

The distinction can be represented as a set of publicly observable evidence states rather than a binary true-or-false label.

The contrast in Table 1 prevents an otherwise tempting category error. A mathematical theorem can be historically established before being formalized; a novel formal proof can exist before its central idea has been reconstructed; a compelling announcement can precede public access to the underlying mathematics. These are different trajectories through the same research ecosystem, and their order need not be fixed. Anthropic’s concurrently announced ETH proof adds another evidence state: a second formalized approach with a separately documented mathematical mechanism and explicit cautions about the absence of human semantic review.

From theorem production to a maintainable mathematical knowledge system

Earlier installments of this series argued that the emerging difficulty is not simply how to generate more solutions. Math 2.0: Beyond the Theorem Factory separated problem selection, exploration, verification, understanding, knowledge preservation, scholarly cooperation, and application. Math 2.0 Gets an Operating System then asked which persistent technical and institutional structures would permit those functions to reinforce one another rather than collapse into a leaderboard of closed conjectures. The October releases put unusually specific mechanisms behind both claims.8788

Axiom demonstrates one possible engineering pattern: map a large body of mathematics into explicitly typed dependencies, schedule work against unresolved proof obligations, integrate the contributions of many agents, and check the result through a common proof kernel. The UGT episode adds an important feedback loop. Translation encounters a false lemma or a missing closure property; mathematical investigation produces a counterexample; the dependency graph identifies affected downstream results; a replacement argument is formalized; exposition then explains the obstruction and may discover a shorter path. Verification becomes a source of new mathematical inquiry rather than merely the final quality-control station.

OpenAI displays a complementary pattern, starting from candidate discoveries rather than established literature. The simultaneous Anthropic result adds competition at the frontier: separate agents may converge on the same previously open problem, while different mechanisms and formalization strategies generate distinct verification obligations. A model supplies an argument, Lean may check a precisely formalized part of it, and a scholarly community must decide whether the proposed statement is faithfully encoded, where it belongs in existing theory, whether its methods generalize, and what should be taught or reused. The failure mode is that an enormous quantity of internally checked but poorly explained work accumulates faster than anyone can absorb it. Public artifacts partly reduce the access problem, while independent exposition addresses a different problem: turning a valid derivation into portable mathematical understanding.

The architecture in Figure 3 combines these trajectories. Its feedback arrows are essential: an error found during formalization can reopen discovery, and an explanatory reconstruction can change the canonical form of an already checked result.

The central engineering challenge is continuity of meaning across transformations. A text theorem becomes a formal statement; an agent converts a proposed argument into proof terms; compilation turns these into a checked dependency closure; a published result becomes a research artifact; later scholars reinterpret it using new methods. An error at any boundary may remain invisible if a system records only the final Boolean fact that a file compiled. Conversely, recording the specification, its evidence, the relationship to earlier work, the relevant counterexamples, and the history of subsequent corrections allows the artifact to acquire scientific value beyond the original run.

This is the sense in which mathematical AI begins to resemble systems engineering without reducing mathematics to software engineering. Dependency management, reproducibility, regression checks, versioning, and release discipline help preserve correctness. They do not determine which classification programme is deep, which auxiliary PDE contains a new principle, or which explanation most fruitfully reorganizes a field. That judgment requires mathematics—sometimes performed by machines, sometimes by humans, and often through their interaction—not merely a better build pipeline.

Why proof is becoming abundant: from embodied expertise to compute-allocable search

Calling the present transition compute-constrained science is suggestive but insufficient. Mathematics has always involved computation in the broad sense of systematic symbolic work, and scientific institutions have long allocated money, equipment, and personnel to promising or prestigious problems. More importantly, access to GPUs is neither necessary nor sufficient for a discovery: a model might fail systematically, repeatedly rediscover known arguments, or prove a specification that does not capture the intended theorem. The emerging phenomenon is better described as compute-allocable mathematical search: after a substantial investment in learned models and proof infrastructure, an organization can assign incremental computation to many research attempts, vary the depth and breadth of each attempt, and process selected outputs through mechanical verification. That is a hypothesis about the production technology of mathematical research, not a claim that computational expenditure has become a reliable substitute for ideas.

Why the historical bottleneck was not simply chance

In much of pure mathematics, frontier progress depended on an unusually specific intersection of skills, accumulated knowledge, sustained attention, and a tractable question. Individual researchers did not receive those abilities by chance alone: universities trained them, mentors and collaborators transmitted methods, publications preserved earlier discoveries, and funders paid for the time in which new questions could be pursued. Nevertheless, the marginal effort needed to explore an unfamiliar proof strategy was largely tied to particular people and their finite working lives. Expertise was comparatively difficult to copy, to split into independent parallel instances, or to redirect on command. A funder could support more mathematicians or a longer programme; it could not immediately create a dozen competent replicas of a rare specialist to try alternate approaches over a weekend.

Even this contrast requires historical qualification. Computer-assisted mathematical work is not new: Appel and Haken’s 1976 four-color theorem drew heavily on roughly 1,200 hours of computer calculations, and the Flyspeck project eventually formalized the computer-assisted Kepler conjecture proof through a substantial human collaboration. Industrial and laboratory science had already moved large parts of inquiry into capital-intensive organizations long before generative AI. The distinctive change concerns the traditionally more cognitively embodied activity of forming lemmas, trying approaches, revising abstractions, and compiling proofs, much of which had resisted multiplication through conventional hardware. The new systems are beginning to make portions of that activity repeatable as computational workloads.8990

The causal mechanism is more specific than additional GPUs

Four mechanisms have to work together. A trained model must possess a usable representation of mathematical concepts and their argumentative structure; large token budgets cannot create this capability from nothing. An agentic controller must convert an open problem into manageable subgoals, revisit failures, and coordinate parallel trajectories. A reliable verifier—often a proof assistant with a small logical kernel—must supply feedback that distinguishes formally successful deductions from unsuccessful ones, enabling search rather than ungrounded text production. Finally, infrastructure must retain intermediate theorems, map dependencies, detect conflicting assumptions, and avoid spending resources endlessly on repeated failures. The interaction is multiplicative in the ordinary causal sense: removing any essential part can drastically lower yield, though no universal numerical product law has been established.

There is evidence for the separate parts of this mechanism, rather than just impressive final announcements. AlphaProof’s published method combines proof search in Lean, reinforcement learning, and extensive test-time exploration; it explicitly increases computational effort by generating and evaluating related problem variants. Goedel-Prover-V2 uses synthetic training problems, compiler feedback, and repeated self-correction while obtaining strong results with comparatively modest open-source models. Rögnvaldsson and collaborators report that a controller deciding when to continue or restart an attempted Lean proof reduced compute expenditure by 25.8% on their tested PutnamBench subset without reducing measured performance. These results matter because they show that the effective variable is not raw hardware quantity but the quality-adjusted yield of a computational search process. More compute can help; better allocation and better verification can also increase useful output at fixed compute.919293

The October cases exhibit that mechanism at different scales, although their public disclosures are not directly comparable performance measurements. Axiom reports about 43.1 million Lean lines produced in 24 days with many agents and human direction. Anthropic’s ETH preprint reports roughly 14 million model output tokens before the core algorithm emerged. OpenAI’s earlier mathematical collection disclosed an average computational budget expressed as the equivalent of approximately three hours of ChatGPT Pro thinking per reported result. These are different accounting units, not interchangeable measures of GPU-hours, dollars, energy, expected solved problems, or scientific value; nor do they reveal the complete distribution of failed searches. They nevertheless show research being organized in explicit computational trajectories rather than solely in the working time of a single mathematician.949596

A simple model for why proof abundance need not imply knowledge abundance

Let B denote the inference budget allocated to a defined research portfolio during a fixed period, and let G(B) denote the number of distinct candidate results generated by a particular model-and-agent system in that period. For this portfolio, write q(B) for the observed fraction of those candidates that are both mathematically correct in their intended interpretation and worth developing further. Let V(H) be the capacity for independent semantic and mathematical validation under a specified allocation H of specialist labour, and let U(H) be the capacity for interpretation, exposition, and incorporation into reusable research knowledge. Under a steady-state assumption, counting unique results rather than repeated attempts, the sustainable rate K of assimilated results is bounded by

K\;\leq\;\min\!\left\{G(B)q(B),\;V(H),\;U(H)\right\}. \tag{6}

This is a capacity bound, not an estimated empirical law. It assumes that candidates flow through these successive obligations, that the units are comparable, and that a one-time backlog is not mistaken for steady-state output; it neither assumes independent errors nor asserts that q stays constant as more compute is spent. Its usefulness is explanatory: even if greater inference budgets increase the candidate rate, the amount of trusted, reusable knowledge cannot increase beyond review and assimilation capacity unless those capacities also change. Conversely, if semantic checking and exposition can themselves be supported by effective machine assistance, their constraints may move as well. The location of the bottleneck is an empirical question, not a permanent assignment of all understanding to humans.

This model also exposes why public announcements supply such weak evidence for the economic claim. We observe selected successes, often without a preregistered problem portfolio, the denominator of failed attempts, the full compute expenditure, or independently confirmed correctness. A rising count of papers or formal files may reflect easier selected targets, duplicated results, longer machine-generated proofs, or improved reporting rather than a comparable rise in mathematical insight. Estimating G, q, V, and U requires measurement on a stable and independently scored problem distribution, not extrapolation from headline achievements. The model should therefore be used to design empirical studies, not to justify claims that a given number of GPUs will produce a given number of breakthroughs.

From scientific priorities to managerial allocation of mathematical attention

The change in production technology nevertheless has immediate implications for scientific agency. Under the older regime, a mathematically trained person could pursue an unpopular problem, even when a funder would rather support something else, because a substantial share of the needed reasoning capability was inseparable from that person’s expertise. Autonomy was never complete; appointments, grants, journals, and intellectual fashions already influenced the research agenda. In the emerging regime, an organization possessing a frontier model, inference infrastructure, and evaluation machinery may instead direct thousands of attempts toward a named result, a benchmark, an application domain, or a publicly spectacular conjecture. The right to allocate compute becomes one of the mechanisms through which a research agenda is chosen.

That does not establish that the recent Yang–Mills, ETH, or CFSG projects were selected for marketing purposes; the public evidence does not support an attribution of motive. It does establish a new possibility of managerial direction that has to be distinguished from mathematical merit. A company could rationally optimize for model evaluation, public demonstration, competitive differentiation, or genuinely useful science, and a single project could serve several objectives. The technical result is judged by proof obligations; the allocation decision is judged by opportunity cost, transparency, and the counterfactual questions that could have been investigated instead. The latter may remain invisible even when every successful Lean proof is published.

AGMAI’s objection therefore reaches upstream of the Yang–Mills release controversy. Its October statements explicitly question laboratory-selected interpreters and the unequal allocation of research agency; its 6 October statement argues that mathematicians should retain the ability to formulate their own questions and calls for equitable access to powerful models and compute. OpenAI’s support for programmes administered by the Simons Foundation is a possible partial response because access and scholarly funding can be distributed beyond the generating laboratory. It does not by itself make inference budgets, agenda-setting, or subsequent validation independent of institutional incentives.979899

The stronger concept is capital-mediated epistemic capacity. A portion of mathematical work becomes an investable, schedulable, and optimizable computational resource, while the competence to design problems, establish meaning, decide what is worth pursuing, and maintain a usable mathematical literature remains embedded in human and institutional practice. This is not the disappearance of expertise but a change in how expertise is combined with productive capital. It also need not entail permanent concentration in a handful of companies: improvements in smaller models, open proof systems, shared infrastructure, or independent compute grants could spread the new capacity. The distribution of that capacity is an institutional variable, not a mathematical theorem.

How to test the proposed shift rather than merely announce it

A decisive study would specify a diversified portfolio of research problems before generating solutions, disclose why those problems were chosen, and vary inference-time budgets within each problem class while holding the model, toolchain, available references, and formal statement fixed. Independent experts would classify the candidates by actual novelty, semantic fidelity, and importance, while the experiment would retain failed attempts, restarts, total compute, and elapsed human intervention. A second arm would vary the quality of orchestration and verifier feedback at a fixed compute budget, separating gains from better systems engineering from gains attributable to more hardware. A third would examine whether researchers outside the originating laboratory can reproduce, explain, and extend the successful results.

The resulting data would distinguish at least three explanations that a headline alone conflates: increasing computational search budget, improving algorithmic or learned reasoning efficiency, and selecting problems unusually amenable to machine verification. It could also measure whether the validation backlog grows as machine output accelerates. Until such comparisons exist, the evidence supports a technologically enabled shift in the allocation and marginal cost of mathematical attempts, not a quantified claim that scientific discovery is now proportional to GPU expenditure. The distinction matters beyond mathematics: in fields where nature rather than a formal kernel must validate a claim, expensive experiments, instruments, samples, and causal inference remain irreducible constraints. Proof abundance is most immediately expected where verification can be made precise, relatively cheap, and mechanically repeatable.

Institutional design now becomes a mathematical constraint

The two announcements also reveal different governance models. Axiom released a large formal repository and a focused human-readable paper explaining one substantive repair. OpenAI, confronting new results whose implications extend across several communities, publicly acknowledged that the manuscripts are provisional inputs to a collective process. Its academic arrangements emphasize transparency, no claim to academic credit for internal model discoveries, acknowledgment of the scholars who develop understanding, and independent, voluntary participation. For the ETH work, OpenAI also agreed to make the materials public and support a community conference.100101102

These commitments address real institutional problems but do not settle them. If a private laboratory controls model access, inference budgets, unpublished intermediate searches, and the timing of scientific releases, it may shape which questions reach public attention even while renouncing formal academic authorship. If the community must explain and evaluate a high-volume stream of outputs, the cost is transferred to a scarce resource: researchers’ attention, careers, and opportunities to formulate their own programmes. Voluntary expert review does not automatically distribute that burden fairly. Conversely, refusing to publish any machine-generated result until complete human exposition exists could conceal useful mathematics and slow independent verification. Different disclosure regimes entail real trade-offs rather than a universal rule.

The physics decision is the sharpest boundary case. The KITP committee has now specified a staged method of semantic review and promised publication of the Lean material before its eventual full assessment. That protocol could avoid giving a false scientific meaning to a formally valid theorem, but it also creates a temporary information asymmetry, while leaving the broader community unable to test the committee’s first question for itself.103 The mathematics and computer-science releases enable wider examination, though public accessibility alone does not guarantee that scholars have the resources to undertake it. A sustainable mathematical commons would need to separate the right to inspect a claim from the power to allocate computational attention, the process of assigning credit from the task of validating deductions, and the storage of a proof from the scholarly work of explaining it.

There is also an economic reason not to measure progress primarily by the number of generated lines or solved conjectures. Axiom’s 43.1 million lines illustrate production scale, not a metric of conceptual compression or future maintainability. A short theorem that makes thousands of pages unnecessary may matter more than a vast checked library; yet a vast library can remain indispensable when no short proof is known. The appropriate measures depend on the scientific objective: independent reconstruction, error detection, transferable techniques, time-to-verification, maintenance cost, and the ability of new researchers to extend the knowledge without returning to the original AI system.

What the October results change in the Math 2.0 thesis

The earlier theorem factory criticism was not that mathematical proofs would become worthless once machines could produce them. It was that proof production could become decoupled from the processes through which mathematical communities formulate useful concepts, preserve intellectual memory, discover new questions, and apply knowledge responsibly. The present evidence makes that concern more precise. Axiom demonstrates that formalization can itself discover obstructions and produce new theorems; OpenAI demonstrates that a formally checked discovery can still arrive as research material in need of explanation. Thus neither formalization nor understanding is properly treated as a passive stage at the end of discovery.104

Four propositions follow, subject to the evidentiary limits above. First, the formal corpus is becoming an active research substrate: proofs can be traversed, repaired, and recombined, and previously dispersed assumptions become explicit objects of mathematical investigation. Second, originality and deductive correctness are no longer adequate joint proxies for scientific assimilation: a checked novelty may have uncertain conceptual status, while a proof of an old theorem may expose a significant new mathematical fact. Third, research governance affects the substance of the knowledge that survives, because disclosure and attribution policies determine which claims can be independently audited, compared, and extended. Fourth, the expansion of machine-search capacity changes the political economy of the discipline: a portion of mathematical exploration can be directed through inference budgets, and the ability to decide which problems receive those budgets becomes a form of scientific agenda-setting.105106

These propositions are testable rather than axiomatic. A multi-institutional team should be able to rebuild and semantically audit the public CFSG development without privileged access to Axiom’s original orchestration. Specialists should be able to reconstruct the core mechanism of the local-smoothing proof, identify the scope of the novel PDE method, and test whether it generates useful extensions. Complexity theorists should be able to trace the ETH result from a conventional model of computation through its formal statement to the precise conditional lower bounds it changes. The quantum-field-theory group should eventually expose constructions and assumptions sufficient for independent evaluation, with the unproved mass-gap problem kept clearly separate. Failure on any one of these tests would narrow the conclusion about scientific progress without necessarily invalidating all the underlying mathematics.

The broader test is longitudinal and now also distributive. Can researchers who do not control a frontier laboratory choose their own questions, obtain credible access to mathematical search capacity, and then reconstruct an argument, identify its decisive lemma, derive a corollary, and teach the method? Can alternative teams retain and reuse the artifacts without the private platform that produced them? Can the system record a corrected proof without erasing evidence of the old defect? If the answer is yes, the system has produced more than proofs: it has helped build durable mathematical knowledge. If the answer is no, even a dramatic increase in solved problems may leave the discipline with a widening deficit of understanding.

Conclusion: when proof ceases to be the endpoint

The announcements of 26 October 2026 should not be merged into a single claim that AI has solved mathematics. Axiom’s case is compelling precisely because it addresses a known theorem whose sheer complexity made comprehensive checking exceptionally difficult, and because the formalization reportedly generated concrete counterexamples and repairs. OpenAI’s case is compelling for an almost opposite reason: it presents potentially transformative new results whose formal artifacts, public accessibility, and community interpretation occupy different stages of maturity. For both, the critical question is not whether an impressive proof has been reported, but what is specified, checked, independently reconstructable, explained, and preserved.

Axiom’s work suggests that the inherited mathematical literature can increasingly be transformed into a checked, maintainable dependency system. OpenAI’s release suggests that the frontier of theorem generation may be advancing faster than the scholarly processes that turn novel results into shared understanding. These developments are complementary, not competing. The former reduces the accumulated uncertainty inside mathematical inheritance; the latter increases the need for institutions capable of processing new claims without surrendering mathematical judgment.

The next phase of Math 2.0 will therefore be measured neither by the number of theorems produced nor by the volume of Lean code compiled. Its economic novelty is that part of mathematical search can increasingly be funded and directed like computational production, even while the meaning and value of its outputs resist reduction to production metrics. The relevant test is whether that capacity supports a research ecology in which questions can be independently chosen and mathematical ideas remain discoverable, verifiable, intelligible, correctable, and reusable by people who were not present when the machines first generated them. The proof is becoming an artifact; mathematical understanding must become a maintained public capability. Under compute-allocable search, this also requires distributed scientific agency: the power to select research problems and contest their purported solutions must not be identical to the power to finance the models that generate them.

Appendix: AGMAI’s critique of privileged access and laboratory-directed mathematical understanding

The argument of this article would be incomplete without a more adversarial reading of OpenAI’s proposed model of academic stewardship. On 26 October 2026, the independent Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence (AGMAI) publicly requested the immediate release of the natural-language and Lean outputs underpinning OpenAI’s announced Yang–Mills, quantum chromodynamics, and Standard Model completion results. The request followed a more extensive statement, published on 25 October, rejecting the practice of AI laboratories designating particular mathematicians to receive, explain, or lead the presentation of AI-generated work. AGMAI’s position is not a mathematical refutation of the results. It is a criticism of the institutional conditions under which claims of mathematical knowledge become inspectable and collectively intelligible—conditions that this article has argued are integral to Math 2.0 rather than incidental to it.107108

What the immediate-release demand actually establishes

AGMAI’s immediate objection is narrowly testable. OpenAI reported that its systems generated a natural-language existence argument for pure Yang–Mills theory on 9 September 2026 and a subsequent QCD argument on 18 September, then completed a Lean formalization of the Yang–Mills existence result on 16 October. Its 26 October announcement nevertheless stated that the physics working group would withhold the AI-generated artifacts while preparing its own exposition. AGMAI points out that the reason OpenAI originally gave for postponement—completion of formalization—no longer applies, by OpenAI’s own account. It therefore requests public disclosure without waiting for the designated group’s write-up.109110

The logical force of this argument must be stated carefully. Completion of a formalization removes one disclosed reason for delay; it does not logically prove that no other reasons for delay could exist. A working group might reasonably need time to diagnose ambiguous statements, improve citations, redact genuinely sensitive information, or avoid premature claims about physical interpretation. But those considerations must be offered as specific, proportionate reasons for withholding source material, not treated as a consequence of the working group’s scholarly authority. In particular, uncertainty about a proof’s interpretation normally favors publishing it as an explicitly unvalidated research artifact, since other qualified researchers may be able to identify its gaps or explain its central ideas. The relevant burden of justification is therefore asymmetric: a consequential existence claim can be announced without asserting that its proof is established, whereas exclusive access prevents outsiders from examining whether the evidence supports even that restricted claim.

The KITP committee’s statement supplies the most serious contemporaneous counterargument, which the immediate-release recommendation cannot simply dismiss as corporate public relations. It reports that the working group intends to examine the equivalence of the physical definitions and formal claims before releasing the Lean development, precisely because an incorrect theorem interpretation or missing scholarly attribution could cause damage. It also promises a sequenced publication: Lean after semantic validation, then a later assessment together with the 1,147-page AI-generated arguments. This constitutes a publicly declared review protocol, although it leaves the timeline open and grants the appointed group a temporary informational advantage. AGMAI is most convincing not when it assumes that a completed Lean run proves release is riskless, but when it asks why semantic checking requires restricting the wider community’s access to the artifact being checked.111112

This is also why a claim of a completed Lean check does not neutralize AGMAI’s objection. When the formal object is unavailable, an external mathematician cannot inspect its statement, imported axioms, definitions of gauge fields and observables, treatment of limits or reconstruction, or correspondence with the natural-language theorem. These are not peripheral implementation details: they determine what was proved. A private statement that a kernel accepted a proof term is evidence about the reporting laboratory’s assertion, not a substitute for an independently reproducible check. The particularly high significance of Yang–Mills theory strengthens the case for rapid inspection without making the claimed existence proof equivalent to a resolution of the separate mass-gap requirement.113

Why choosing the interpreters changes the epistemic process

The deeper AGMAI criticism survives even when the AI outputs are public. Its 25 October statement identifies several distinct mechanisms: early or unequal access can give selected researchers a head start; the laboratory’s designation of an interpretive leader can diminish other mathematicians’ agency; selection reflects company networks and priorities; and the public association of prominent names with an announcement can appear to confer credibility before those scholars have completed any verification. AGMAI additionally argues that community-organized reading groups, independent papers, and workshops make exclusive designation unnecessary. These claims have different evidentiary status: the existence of preferential access or company selection can be documented, whereas its chilling effect on other researchers and the alleged sufficiency of organic coordination are plausible institutional hypotheses whose magnitude is not established merely by the statement.114

One distinction is especially important. An expert can be independent in the conclusions they reach while remaining dependent in the opportunity to reach them first. OpenAI promises that participation in its collaborations is voluntary and unpaid, imposes no nondisclosure agreements, and disclaims both academic credit for model discoveries and ownership of the participants’ judgments. Those commitments reduce certain risks of capture; they do not remove the asymmetry produced by selecting the initial experts, routing materials through them, or allowing one group to determine the timing of a public release. OpenAI itself acknowledges that choosing initial scholarly shepherds shapes who participates first and creates unequal access to information. AGMAI’s disagreement is thus not with the personal integrity of the scientists involved but with the institution that assigns them a privileged epistemic position.115116

The concern about borrowed reputation is particularly subtle. Mathematical correctness cannot be inferred from a participant’s standing: a theorem is established by a valid derivation of its actual proposition from acceptable premises, not by the prestige of those invited to read it. Yet names prominently associated with a release can influence journalists, funding bodies, hiring committees, and researchers deciding whether to invest scarce attention. If those audiences conflate being selected to investigate with having endorsed the result, reputational authority can be transferred to an unexamined claim without any deliberate endorsement by the mathematicians. A responsible release must therefore distinguish, in its language and presentation, the producer of an artifact, the person who agrees to study it, the person who independently verifies it, and the author of a later explanatory reconstruction.

Applying the criticism to OpenAI’s three release strategies

OpenAI’s October announcement provides a controlled comparison of three institutional arrangements. For local smoothing, it released both natural-language and Lean materials and left the mathematical community free to organize its own interpretive work. For the ETH claim, it released both artifacts while also convening a designated group of complexity theorists to help organize scrutiny and explanation. For quantum field theory, it designated a working group and deferred publication of the underlying AI materials. These approaches are not equivalent under AGMAI’s criteria. The first permits inspection and open participation from the outset; the second permits inspection but leaves questions about the signalling and agenda-setting effects of company-designated leadership; the third adds an exclusion from the evidentiary record itself.117118

The distinction also exposes a weakness in treating academic stewardship as a single governance solution. Expert coordination, independent validation, public access, and public exposition solve different problems. A designated group may provide excellent coordination without earning the authority to restrict the corpus it is coordinating; a public repository may provide excellent access without guaranteeing that any qualified group has time to understand what is posted. Conversely, simultaneous publication should not be confused with equal practical opportunity. Researchers with access to larger compute budgets, automated proof-search systems, or funded teams can still advance faster than those restricted to reading the artifact. AGMAI’s 29 September recommendations address precisely this broader asymmetry, arguing that proprietary model access and unequal research resources risk producing a stratified mathematical community.119

The near-concurrent OpenAI and Anthropic ETH releases add a further reason not to conflate laboratory publicity with communal validation. A competitive announcement may rationally be made as soon as a checked artifact is ready, especially when a rival laboratory is working on the same problem. The epistemic response cannot be to forbid parallel research or pretend that such competition is absent. It is to make the resulting claims equally inspectable, preserve versioned provenance and credit, and prevent publication priority from being mistaken for correctness or depth. Anthropic’s detailed public admission of the distance between Lean checking and human interpretation illustrates a useful disclosure norm, even though it does not independently establish the correctness of the result.120

A serious objection to AGMAI—and what survives it

The strongest objection to AGMAI is not that experts should be trusted by default, but that immediately releasing a technically immature result can create a large verification burden. A poorly annotated proof, released with dramatic claims of discovery, may lead dozens of researchers to duplicate elementary checks, chase mistranslations, or expend attention on a result whose significance was overstated. Controlled, expert-led preparation might improve the initial theorem statement, expose hidden assumptions, avoid confusing conjectural physics with proved mathematics, and reduce the social cost of reviewing raw output. Moreover, AGMAI’s broader preference that laboratories stop testing frontier mathematical problems on proprietary models is a normative position about the organization of science, not a conclusion compelled by mathematical logic; it would need to be defended against the potential scientific benefit of using otherwise unavailable computational capabilities.121

These objections are substantial, but they do not require exclusive access until exposition is complete. The alternatives are not limited to either unqualified publication or indefinite private review. A lab can publish a prominently provisional result with its exact target statement, proof status, unresolved interpretive questions, build environment, and known limitations, while independently funding mathematical groups to produce readable explanations. It can release an initial version and later attach corrected versions without silently replacing the record. For outputs whose publication creates material risks beyond scholarly confusion, narrowly specified restrictions may be defensible; no such rationale follows automatically from the desire to produce a polished mathematics paper. Open availability and epistemic caution are compatible if the release states exactly what has and has not been checked.

Nor should AGMAI’s recommendation be transformed into a demand that the generating laboratory surrender every proprietary model weight or expose confidential infrastructure. Open inspection of a mathematical result and equal access to the system that generated it are related but separable policies. The former permits independent scrutiny of an existing claim; the latter determines who can generate further frontier claims and is constrained by additional technical, economic, and governance considerations. The feasibility of exact experimental reproduction may depend on inaccessible models, but the reproducibility of a Lean verification should not depend on the original generative run. That distinction helps identify what can reasonably be demanded as a minimum today without pretending that full equality of computational resources has already been secured.

From criticism to a publication and assurance protocol

AGMAI’s September recommendations provide the starting point for a concrete protocol, not merely a declaration of values. They distinguish results understood and responsibly authored by humans, which should proceed through ordinary scholarly preprints and peer review, from AI output whose arguments nobody yet fully understands. For the latter they call for prompt deposit in repositories independent of the laboratory, persistent identifiers and revision histories, substantial effort at literature attribution and readable exposition, a declared formalization status, and provenance describing the model, prompts, attempts, compute, cost, and problem-selection process. They also recommend that support for human understanding be funded by laboratories but allocated through independent nonprofit institutions. In this model, financing scholarly scrutiny does not buy the laboratory authority to decide what the community should conclude.122123

An implementable assurance package would separate three layers. At the proof layer, it would publish the exact formal target, source files, toolchain pinning, axioms and external certificates, build instructions, and checks sufficient for third parties to validate the dependency closure. At the interpretation layer, it would provide a mapping between natural-language assertions and formal statements, an explanation of definitions and domain restrictions, and an explicit record of claims for which that mapping is unverified. At the research-process layer, it would record versioned provenance, sampling or search attempts and failures where disclosable, literature links, known corrections, and a transparent route for independent challenges. A formalization certificate without an interpretation layer risks proving the wrong proposition; a polished exposition without the proof layer can obscure deductive gaps; and both without provenance can misrepresent how representative the result is of the generating system’s performance.

OpenAI’s announced support for independent academic work, including funding routed through the Simons Foundation, is relevant because interpretation is labor-intensive and should not be treated as a free service supplied by the wider research community. Nevertheless, accountability should be evaluated by observable mechanisms rather than by the existence of funding alone: who chooses the problems, who controls the repository, who grants early access, who decides what corrections are visible, and who can publicly disagree without losing access or support? The answers determine whether an apparent mathematical commons remains dependent on a proprietary center.124125

The lasting significance of AGMAI’s objection for this series is therefore sharper than a demand for transparency. If machine-produced mathematics becomes abundant, the scarce resource will be not only human understanding but also the independence of the processes that produce it. Mathematical knowledge cannot function as a shared, cumulative system when the same institution controls frontier generation, selective disclosure, privileged interpretation, and the public framing of its own claims. AGMAI has not proved that every designated working group will distort mathematics, nor that unrestricted release always maximizes scientific welfare. It has identified a structural concentration of powers for which no Lean proof can supply an institutional remedy. Math 2.0 requires not merely a reliable theorem compiler, but a publicly contestable process for deciding which proofs become knowledge.
